Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of vision loss in older adults, and its **early signs are often subtle**: small drusen, pigmentary changes and reticular pseudodrusen that can be difficult to appreciate on a single white-light image.

This case framing illustrates **how multispectral imaging can help visualize** these features. It is not a diagnostic protocol, and all clinical interpretation belongs to the treating professional. *[TO CONFIRM: any device-specific imaging and any patient images, with consent.]*

## What different bands can emphasize

- **Green–yellow bands** can enhance the contrast of superficial features and the macular region.
- **Red and near-infrared bands** penetrate deeper and may help reveal **sub-RPE and choroidal-level** contrast where deposits and pigmentary change sit. *[TO CONFIRM: wavelength-to-structure mapping.]*
- **Reticular pseudodrusen**, which carry prognostic significance, are an example of a feature that can be challenging to see in a single composite image but may stand out when bands are compared.

## Why this matters for follow-up

Earlier, clearer visualization supports better **documentation and monitoring over time** — establishing a richer baseline against which change can be tracked. Multispectral imaging contributes to that record; it does not replace OCT, autofluorescence or the clinician's judgment.

> The value is a more informative photograph and a more durable baseline — captured in one sitting.

## The responsible framing

Throughout, the language is deliberate: multispectral imaging **helps visualize** and **supports documentation**. It does not, by itself, diagnose AMD or determine treatment. Any quantitative or AI-assisted AMD capability would be separately validated and labeled.
