A standard color fundus photograph captures the retina the way the eye sees it — as a single blend of red, green and blue light reflected back from a complex, layered tissue. It is fast, familiar and clinically invaluable. But it also compresses a great deal of information into three broad channels.

Multispectral imaging (MSI) takes a different approach: it illuminates the retina with a **sequence of narrow wavelength bands**, from shorter (blue–green) wavelengths through to longer red and near-infrared light, and records a separate image at each.

## Why wavelength matters

Light of different wavelengths penetrates the retina to different depths and is absorbed differently by the eye's natural chromophores — hemoglobin in blood, melanin in the pigment epithelium and choroid, and macular pigment. As a result:

- **Shorter wavelengths** tend to emphasize structures nearer the inner retinal surface.
- **Longer, red and near-infrared wavelengths** penetrate deeper, carrying information from the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and choroid. *[TO CONFIRM: exact wavelength-to-depth mapping for any specific device.]*

By comparing the same location across bands, MSI can make certain features — drusen, sub-retinal changes, vascular and pigmentary contrast — easier to visualize than in a single composite image.

## Not a replacement — a richer record

It is important to frame MSI honestly. Multispectral imaging **helps visualize** structures and contrast; it does not, on its own, diagnose disease. It is complementary to established modalities such as OCT, which resolves cross-sectional retinal layers, and angiography, which maps blood flow. The clinician remains the decision-maker.

What MSI adds is a **denser, depth-aware photographic record** — and, increasingly, a substrate that modern AI can learn from across the full spectrum rather than three color channels.

> The promise of spectral imaging is not a prettier picture. It is more measurable signal per capture — and a record you can revisit as the science matures.
