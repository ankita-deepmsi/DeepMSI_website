Artificial intelligence in ophthalmology has moved from research curiosity to deployed clinical tool. Three threads define the current moment.

## 1. Autonomous screening goes mainstream

Several autonomous AI systems for **diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening** have received regulatory clearance and entered primary-care and retail-clinic settings, where they grade fundus images and flag patients who need referral. Reimbursement pathways (such as CPT code 92229 in the United States) have helped accelerate adoption. *[TO CONFIRM: specific products, clearance numbers and performance figures before citing.]*

The clinical value proposition is access: bringing a first-line retinal check to settings without an on-site ophthalmologist.

## 2. Foundation models for the retina

Following the pattern set elsewhere in AI, the field is moving toward **foundation models** — large models pre-trained on enormous volumes of retinal images that can then be adapted to many downstream tasks with comparatively little labeled data. Publicly described efforts have shown that such models can generalize across disease-detection and even systemic-prediction tasks.

This matters for spectral imaging: a richer, multi-wavelength input gives a foundation model more signal to learn from than three color channels.

## 3. Oculomics — the retina as a systemic biomarker

"Oculomics" describes using retinal imaging to infer signals about **systemic health** — cardiovascular risk, kidney function and neurological conditions among them. The research is genuinely promising and genuinely early; most systemic-prediction claims remain investigational and require larger, more diverse validation. *[TO CONFIRM: all systemic-disease claims.]*
